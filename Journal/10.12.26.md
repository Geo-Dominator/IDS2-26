## My Thoughts
This chapter was mostly about constraints, which considering the chapter was titled "Constraints, Discoverability, and Feedback", I was expecting a bit more in the other two but whatever. To summarize: your design needs to be immediate, informative, and prioritized. Too little feedback leaves users guessing; too much creates noise and anxiety. To do so, you need to constraint your creativity a little. Think outside the box on how people tend to think inside the box. Make something that even Patrick Star could use. Because if he can use it, anyone can.

### Constraints
There are 4 types of constraints, dividing this Journal up even further. But basically all of them summarize how Systems need people to think less to succeed. If they have to memorize how things work, you're doing it wrong.
1. **Physical** - Limitations based on physical shapes or features
While reading this, all I can think of was this one video of a girl becoming more and more distraught as someone put all the shaped pegs into the square hole. I'll post a link [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cUbIkNUFs-4). But yeah, I felt like this constraint made the most sense. I would say the biggest contender of something that failed this constraint was USB cables. I mean, it's why I made it the image in the first place. I'm so glad they upgraded the next USB to be double sided, eliminating that pain. But whenever I have to move my pc around, with it's 8 usb ports, trying to get behind a desk... just thinking about it... a single tear runs down my cheek.
2. **Cultural** - Allowed or preferred behavior dictated by a society's shared conventions
Our world is a vast and diverse place, where complete with a diverse collection of cultures. And in many of these cultures, things tend to seem contradictory and off. For example, how you read, how you drive, how you present yourself, and how you eat are all vastly different depending on where on Earth you're at at a given moment. And because things can feel contradictory in one area but correct in another, you have to take into consideration all of these areas. I can think of two scenarios: instruction booklets in every single language, and how video games will have an EU version as well as an American version.
3. **Semantic** - Limitations based on the meaning of the situation
Ever heard of Occam's razor? It's the idea that a solution to a problem is usually (or should be) the easiest one. I think that's Semantic Constraints in a nutshell to be honest. You need the details to the point of redundancy. Helmets made so it's the most comfortable in a certain, the correct, position. Buttons that beep on a mircowave and a digital response just in case as well. holding a pencil one was creates lines, flipping it around removes lines. Nuff Said.
4. *Logical - Working out the relationships naturally using spatial or functional logic
An example given was light switches. Those aren't labelled, and some can do multiple lights, or just one. Compare that to something like a stovetop. There's 4 knobs and 4 burners, so logically there should be a knob connected to a burner. But with light switches, you don't have that connection. I remember in my childhood bedroom it had one light switch in it, but no celling light. So what did it turn on and off? the outlet on the left wall only. There were many times where my phone died because someone flipped that switch unknowingly, trying to turn on and off a light that wasn't there.

### Forcing Functions
A special type of physical constraint where an action must be done before another can proceed. Some examples given would include things like Microwaves stopping when you open them, cars not starting when you press the break. Some examples that I can think of while reading were circuit breakers. When you overload the grid, breakers are designed to prevent damage and surges. While asking a friend, they brought up Headphones they used to have that when it detected you taking them off they'd pause or mute whatever you were listening to.

### Feedback
There are two situations where user designs struggle the most, that being the Gulf of Execution and the Gulf of Evaluation. However, there's a simple solution to tell the user info during these gulfs to "bridge" over the water. And that... is simply telling the user info. I remember when in Webside design 2 when learning Javascript wor website making, a lot of if statements ended with an else that stated console.log("An error has occurred"). We were taught early on whenever we tried to do something, just in case make an error code specific to the code you're working on. Because not only did it make it easy for us to see what code was broken, it showed the user that they didn't do anything wrong, my code was just terrible.

## Favorite Quote from the chapter

"We learn arbitrary associations or sequences by artificially providing structure. Most books and courses on methods for improving memory (mnemonics) use a variety of standard methods for providing structure, even for things that might appear completely arbitrary, such as grocery lists, or matching the names of people to their appearance. As we saw in the discussion of these methods for STM, even strings of digits can be remembered if they can be associated with meaningful structures. People who have not received this training or who have not invented some methods themselves often try to manufacture some artificial structure, but these are often rather unsatisfactory, which is why the learning is so bad." (98-99)
